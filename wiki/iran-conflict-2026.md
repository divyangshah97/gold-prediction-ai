# Iran Conflict 2026

**Summary**: The 2026 US-Israel military strike on Iran and subsequent ceasefire was the single largest acute geopolitical shock to gold markets in 2026, driving prices from ~$4,000 to $5,200. As of June 9, a fresh Iran-Israel direct military exchange occurred (IDF struck Beirut June 7; Iran launched ballistic missiles at Israel) — the first direct exchange since the April ceasefire — before both sides agreed to halt attacks on June 9. Nuclear talks by June 30 are increasingly unlikely (75.5% against per market consensus). Gold at $4,338.50, recovering from June 8's $4,329 low. WTI $91.28/bbl (eased on ceasefire).

## Market Impact

### Oil & Energy

The Strait of Hormuz — through which ~20% of global oil passes — was reduced to approximately **5% of pre-conflict shipping traffic** at peak disruption. This remains the case as of May 2026, well after the ceasefire.

Sustained Hormuz disruption is a key driver of **energy-driven inflation** that is complicating the Fed's rate path (see [[fed-macro-factors]]). The ceasefire held the conflict, but did not restore shipping traffic.

### Gold Price Reaction

Gold's move during the Iran crisis illustrates the [[gold-geopolitical-risk-premium]] mechanism in an extreme form:

- **Pre-conflict baseline:** Gold was trading ~$4,000 (early 2026)
- **Crisis peak:** ~$5,200/oz (March 2026, during peak Hormuz disruption and oil spike)
- **Post-ceasefire:** Retreated to ~$5,000, then corrected further to ~$4,500–4,540 (May 2026)

The combined post-ceasefire + post-Trump/Xi-summit correction brought gold down ~$650–700/oz from its peak. The structural floor — supported by [[central-bank-gold-demand]] — held well above the 200-day SMA at $4,341.

## Nuclear Negotiations — May 2026 (Updated May 25)

### Status

As of May 24, 2026, US-Iran talks have progressed significantly:
- Both sides are "closing in" on a **memorandum of understanding (MOU)** — a roadmap for resolving all outstanding issues (CNN, May 24)
- The draft MOU includes: 60-day ceasefire extension; Strait of Hormuz reopening; Iran permitted to sell oil freely; negotiations on curbing uranium enrichment program (Axios, May 24)
- Iran committed in the draft to "never pursue nuclear weapons" (Axios)
- **Pakistan and Qatar** are serving as active mediators, relaying proposals between Washington and Tehran

### Remaining Sticking Points

1. **Timing of sanctions relief** — Iran wants simultaneous relief; US wants sequenced with verification
2. **Strait of Hormuz control** — precise terms of the navigation guarantee
3. **Uranium stockpile** — Iran's position: enriched uranium must not leave Iranian soil; draft MOU language still being negotiated

### Prior Escalation Context (May 21–22)

On May 21, Iran's new Supreme Leader **Mojtaba Khamenei** (son of Ali Khamenei, killed February 28) issued a directive that Iran's enriched uranium must not leave Iranian soil. Trump responded by threatening resumed military strikes "in a couple more days." Gulf Arab allies persuaded Trump to hold off. The 72-hour reversal to a near-deal is a significant development.

**Gold market implication**: With MOU negotiations active and likely resolution approaching, the primary near-term risk-premium catalyst is diminishing. However, gold's response has been *positive* (up $40–50) because the market is pricing the Fed/inflation implications of Hormuz reopening (lower oil → easier Fed → weaker USD → higher gold) rather than simply the safe-haven compression. If a deal stalls or collapses, re-escalation could push gold toward $5,000+; if signed, the risk premium component ($100–300/oz) may unwind but structural demand support limits the downside.

## Structural Implications

1. **Energy inflation persistence:** Hormuz traffic at 5% means global energy supply chains remain stressed. This feeds directly into the Fed's inability to cut rates (see [[fed-macro-factors]]). Oil at $104+ is stoking inflation fears that complicate rate-cut timing.

2. **Safe-haven demand context:** Gold's ~1% dip on May 22 (to ~$4,507–$4,524) reflects a push-pull: the uranium directive is bearish via oil/inflation/rates, but bullish via safe-haven demand. Net effect was modest decline.

3. **Central bank buying unaffected:** [[central-bank-gold-demand]] continued through the crisis — price-inelastic buyers like Poland and China did not reduce purchases during the spike or the correction.
