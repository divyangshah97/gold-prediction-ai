# Canada Gold Reserves

**Summary**: Canada holds **zero tonnes** of gold — the only G7 country to have completely liquidated its gold reserves. Canada sold its final 0.62 tonnes in early 2016, completing a 36-year sell-off from a peak of 1,023 tonnes in 1965. At today's gold prices (~$4,500/oz), that 1,023t would be worth ~US$132 billion.

## The Sell-Off Timeline

| Period | Event |
|---|---|
| 1965 | Peak reserves: 1,023 tonnes |
| 1970s | Policy shift begins — gold seen as non-performing asset post-Bretton Woods |
| 1980 | Formal Bank of Canada policy to sell gold adopted |
| 1980–2002 | Systematic selling — by mid-1990s, ~90% of 1965 peak was gone |
| 2002–2016 | Final small holdings wound down |
| February 2016 | **Last 0.62 tonnes sold — reserves hit zero** |

## Why Canada Sold Everything

The Canadian government's rationale, articulated by the Finance Department and supported by the Bank of Canada:

1. **"Non-performing asset"**: Gold pays no interest or dividends. Finance economists argued that bonds and equities generate returns; gold just sits there
2. **Storage costs**: Physically securing and insuring gold costs money — seen as inefficient
3. **Post-Bretton Woods logic**: Once the gold standard ended (1971), there was no monetary requirement to hold gold — it became "just a commodity"
4. **Liquidity preference**: Government policy favoured reserves in "easily tradable financial assets with deep markets" — USD, EUR, GBP bonds
5. **Canada's unique position**: Canada is a major gold **producer** (5th–8th globally depending on year); the government may have felt exposure to gold was already built in through the domestic mining sector

Former Finance economist **Don Drummond** argued that gold yielded poor long-term returns and was costly to store — a view that looked defensible throughout the 1980s–1990s bear market but became costly in hindsight.

---

## The Opportunity Cost

At ~$4,500/oz (May 2026):
- Canada's 1,023t peak would be worth **~US$132 billion**
- The gold was sold at average prices between roughly $300–$600/oz across different decades
- The assets purchased with the proceeds (bonds, currency deposits) performed well in the 1990s–2000s but have since been dwarfed by gold's price appreciation

Canada missed the entire gold bull market of 2001–2011 (gold rose from ~$270 to $1,900+) and the 2018–2026 institutional accumulation cycle.

---

## Political Controversy (2025–2026)

As gold hit all-time highs (~$5,200/oz in March 2026), Canadian politicians and commentators revived the debate:
- Opposition parties cited the zero-gold policy as a costly blunder
- Some analysts calculated the loss at **US$130+ billion** relative to holding
- The Bank of Canada and Finance Department have not signalled any intent to rebuild gold reserves
- A false claim circulated (fact-checked in 2025) that PM Mark Carney "sold gold to the UK" — this confused the reserve sell-off (which predated Carney) with mineral exports

There is currently **no policy proposal to buy gold** from any major Canadian political party.

---

## Significance for Gold Price Prediction

Canada's position is a **cautionary tale** rather than a market signal:
- Canada is not a buyer or seller — it has nothing to sell
- The story illustrates how dramatically central bank consensus can be wrong on gold
- It also shows why [[de-dollarisation]]-driven EM buyers are unlikely to follow Canada's path — the Russia 2022 sanctions proved that non-Western countries *must* hold non-confiscatable assets

If Canada ever reversed its zero-gold policy and began buying (unlikely short-term), it would be a significant bullish signal — 1,023t of demand entering the market over several years.
